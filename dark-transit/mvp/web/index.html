Radar sees oil
as absence.

Wind roughens the sea, and a rough sea throws energy back at the satellite. Oil flattens the surface, so the energy scatters away and never returns.

The slick arrives as a hole in the image. That is the whole detection principle — and the whole difficulty, because calm water is a hole too, and so is an algal bloom, and so is the wind shadow behind a headland.

Forty hours,
run backward.

Oil moves with the current plus roughly three percent of the wind. Seed the slick with particles and step them back an hour at a time. The patch walks west-southwest toward wherever it was poured.

It does not converge on a point. It spreads. Every hour of rewind compounds the error in the current and wind fields, so the honest output is a region and a window — growing as you go, and reported that way.

Nine hulls
crossed that water.

Every large ship broadcasts position, course and speed, continuously, to anyone listening. Replay the window and the region fills with tracks.

Heading does most of the clearing. A discharge made under way lays the film along the track — so the slick's long axis is a bearing, and a vessel whose course disagrees with it by forty degrees did not make it.

And then nothing.

At — the transponder goes quiet. At — it resumes, well past the region, as though nothing had happened in between.

This is not missing data. It is the finding. Equipment fails and coverage thins offshore — but it rarely fails on approach and recovers on departure.
